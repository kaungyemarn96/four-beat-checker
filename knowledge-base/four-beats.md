# The four beats, and how each one fails

Loaded when a beat is graded `weak` or `missing`. Not needed for a clean pass.

Current as of: August 2026.

---

## 1. Wrong question

**What it does:** names the assumption the reader walked in with, and shows it
is the wrong thing to be asking. Not a hook, not a promise — a correction.

**Diagnosis questions**
- Can you point at the sentence that states what the reader believed?
- Is it a belief a real person holds, or a strawman nobody argues for?
- Does the piece show it is wrong, or merely assert it?

**How it fails**
- *Missing entirely.* The piece opens on the answer. Nothing is at stake,
  because no wrong belief has been put on the table to be dislodged.
- *Too easy.* The wrong question is one no reader actually holds, so correcting
  it costs the reader nothing and teaches them nothing.
- *Question without a questioner.* Stated abstractly ("many people wonder…")
  instead of as the reader's own assumption.
- *Buried.* Arrives in paragraph nine, after the escape hatch has already been
  given away.

**Sign it is working:** a reader who disagrees with the correction keeps reading
to find out why they are wrong.

---

## 2. Escape hatch

**What it does:** the real mechanism, tool, or move that the better question
leads to. The load-bearing beat — everything before sets it up, everything
after proves it.

**Diagnosis questions**
- Is there a specific named thing, or a category of things?
- Could a reader repeat it back accurately after one read?
- Does it follow from the corrected question, or is it a change of subject?

**How it fails**
- *Category, not mechanism.* "Better systems" instead of the specific move.
- *Unearned.* The mechanism is stated but not connected to the wrong question
  it was supposed to answer, so it reads as a non-sequitur.
- *Plural.* Three escape hatches is none. A reader cannot act on a menu.
- *Withheld.* Teased and deferred to the conclusion. The beat has to land in the
  body, not at the end.

**Sign it is working:** a reader could describe the mechanism to a colleague
without re-reading.

---

## 3. Who actually uses it

**What it does:** real people, real numbers, woven into prose. Turns a claim
into an observed fact.

**Diagnosis questions**
- Are there named actors, or only "companies" and "teams"?
- Are the numbers traceable to a source stated in the prose?
- Are they in sentences, or dropped as a stat block?

**How it fails**
- *Unsourced numbers.* The fastest way to lose a reader who checks. If a figure
  cannot be traced, cut it or soften it to a hedged range.
- *Stat sheet.* Figures listed rather than argued with. Numbers belong inside
  sentences that do something with them.
- *Anonymous actors.* "A major operator" carries a fraction of the weight of a
  named one, and the difference is what the beat is for.
- *One example doing all the work.* Reusing the same company or country as the
  pivot twice reads as the only case that exists.

**Sign it is working:** a sceptical reader stops being sceptical without being
told to.

---

## 4. Your turn

**What it does:** what the reader does now, concrete enough to start today.

**Diagnosis questions**
- Is there a first action, and could it be done this week?
- Does it use the mechanism from beat 2, or drift to general advice?
- Does the piece end on something concrete, or on encouragement?

**How it fails**
- *Summary in disguise.* Restates the article instead of handing over an action.
  A closing summary is not this beat.
- *Vague optimism.* "Start thinking differently about your content." Nothing to
  do on Monday.
- *Pitch.* The action is to hire someone. That is a different document.
- *Disconnected.* Advice that would be reasonable in any article, which means it
  did not come from this one.

**Sign it is working:** the reader can name their first step without scrolling
back up.

**On houses that ban hollow conclusions.** Many style guides, including the one
this package came from, tell writers to end on a concrete fact or a tension and
never on encouragement. That rule and this beat are not in conflict, and reading
them as a conflict is the most common reason a good draft ships without a beat 4.

A specific action *is* a concrete fact. "Open your CMS and check whether a single
page emits Organization schema" ends on something the reader can verify. What the
rule prohibits is hollow encouragement, not instruction. The test is whether the
last sentence could be checked by someone doing it: if it can, the beat and the
house rule are both satisfied, and grading it `missing` is an error.

Grade `missing` only when there is no action at all. Do not grade it `missing`
because the piece ends on tension rather than a summary.

---

## Order failures

The beats can all exist and still fail, in three recognisable ways:

- **Escape hatch before wrong question.** The mechanism arrives before the
  reader knows why they need it, so it reads as an assertion.
- **Proof before mechanism.** Numbers land before the thing they prove, and the
  reader carries figures with nothing to attach them to.
- **Your turn before proof.** The action is asked for before the case is made,
  so a sceptical reader has already stopped.

In every case the fix is a move, not a rewrite. Say which block goes where.
